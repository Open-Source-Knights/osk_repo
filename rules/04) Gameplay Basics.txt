



WHAT DO I NEED TO PLAY?
	Like in most pen-and-paper TTRPGs, characters are represented primarily by their character sheet. 
	The character sheet records your character's stats, such as Attributes (STR/DEX/CON/INT/WIS/CHA), Skills, health (Hit Points), and other essential information.
	However, all character abilities (such as species traits, fighting maneuvers, and spells) are all represented on 3x5 notecards, created by the player.

WHAT TYPES OF DICE DOES OSK USE?
	OSK uses: a d20, ten d6, and a d100 (typically represented by a d10 and a percentile die)
	OSK DOES NOT use: d4s, d8s, or d12s

TIMEOUT:
	At any point during the game, any player (including the GM) can call a Timeout.
	During a Timeout, gameplay immediately pauses, and all players break character and discuss the situation out of game.
	Timeouts can be used to ask questions and get clarifications as necessary.
	Timeouts can also be used if a situation in the game is making a player feel uncomfortable out of game.
	When the issue or question has been resolved to everyone's satisfaction, gameplay resumes as usual.

CHARACTER SHEET AND CARDS:
	The sheet represents most of the characteristics that make up your character.
	Several areas of the sheet have circular pips that represent things like Experience, Attriubtes, Recovery Points, etc. These pips are always bubbled in from left-to-right.
	Your character's species, abilities, equipment, and supplies are represented by a deck of 3x5 notecards that you create as you build your character.
	You create additional cards as your character adventures and gains experience and new equipment.

CARD TYPES:
	There are four types of cards in OSK.
	Species - a card that represents the traits of your character's species.
	Abilities - a card that represents a character's combat maneuver, spell, or other feature.
	Equipment - a card that represents a piece of equipment, such as a special weapon or tool.
	Consumable - a single-use card, such as a potion. It is removed from your deck after it is used.
	It is recommended that you assign a color for each type of card.
	OSK's default coloring is: Species/Red, Ability/Blue, Equipment/Green, Consumable/Purple.

DECK & LOADOUT:
	Your "Deck" is all of the 3x5 cards your character has available to them. 
	There is no limit on the number of cards you can have in your deck.
	Your "Loadout" is a subset of 10 cards that your character has access to on your turn in combat.
	Note: Your character's Species card is always available to you, but it DOES NOT count against your Loadout's 10-card limit.
	The purpose of the Loadout is to prevent excessive cognitive load on players.
	Outside of combat, you can swap cards between your Deck and Loadout at will, as often as you want.
	To swap cards between your Deck and Loadout after combat has begun, you must take the Reconfigure action (see Chapter XX).

HIT POINTS:
	Your character's Hit Points (HP) represent how far they are from death.
	As your character takes wounds and sustains injuries their HP decreases.
	However, HP does not necessarily represent only wounds - it can also represent the stamina a character has left to avoid fatal blows.
	HP can be regained via the application of first aid, medicine, potions, healing spells, etc.
	Once your character's HP reaches 0, they fall unconscious and must begin making Death Saving Throws.

DEATH / DEATH SAVING THROWS:
	To make a Death Saving Throw, simply roll a d20 and add nothing. If the roll is 10 or greater, mark one success. If the roll is 9 or less, mark one failure.
	During combat, a dying character must make a Death Saving Throw after EVERY time a Player Team turn concludes.
	If a character takes damage while they are dying, they automatically fail one additional death save.
	If your character succeeds on 3 Death Saving Throws, they "stabilize" and regain 1 HP (this does not cost Recovery Points, and a character can stabilize even if they are out of Recovery Points).
	If your character fails 3 Death Saving Throws, they die.
	Depending on the circumstances of your game and campaign, you may be able to resurrect them.
	If resurrection is not possible, it's time to create a new character!

TEMPORARY HIT POINTS:
	Temporary HP represents a short-lived buffer against damage.
	Taking damage reduces your Temporary HP before reducing your normal HP.
	Temporary HP cannot be restored through healing.
	Multiple effects that grant Temporary HP can be stacked, but you cannot stack the same effect multiple times. 
	Temporary HP usually disappears after a Long Rest, or when the effect granting it ends.

RECOVERY POINTS:
	Your character can regain lost HP by application of first aid, medicines, potions, healing spells, etc.
	Every character is capable of healing themselves and others, by using First Aid: make a Medicine check and using the result as the amount of HP restored.
	Every time your character regains any amount of HP, you mark one Recovery Point off your character sheet.
	Every character has 10 Recovery Points. When these are exhausted, your character can no longer regain HP until they rest.
	However, a character without any Recovery Points can still be stabilized (brought up to 1 HP) if they are dying.
	Certain rare consumables can allow characters to regain Recovery Points between rests.
	Resting restores all 10 of a character's Recovery Points.

RESTING:
	A Long Rest (also called a "Respite" or simply a "Rest") is an 8-12 hour period where your character does little more than sleep.
	Resting restores all of a character's HP, as well as all of their ecovery Points. It also removes the "Fatigued" condition.
	Note: There are no "short rests" - resting requires a minimum of 8 hours for most characters.

DAMAGE TYPES:
	Damage from abilities/equipment/etc is categorized by type:
	Physical	Slashing, piercing, or bludgeoning damage
	Acid		Damage from corrosive liquids
	Cold		Ice and frost effects, hypothermia
	Fire		Damage from heat, combustion, and certain types of explosions
	Lightning	Electricity-based damage, whether natural or artificial
	Necrotic	Sapping a creature's life force, causing decay
	Poison		Venomous stings and toxic gases
	Psychic		Psionic attacks, psychological manipulation, or extreme exertions of willpower
	Radiant		Damage inflicted by holy energy or divine light
	Sonic		Damage from concussive blasts and loud sounds

DAMAGE VULNERABILITY / RESISTANCE / IMMUNITY:
	Some creatures take greater or lesser amounts of damage from attacks, based on the damage type.
	Vulnerability - A creature takes double damage of the specified type
	Resistance - A creature takes half damage of the specified type
	Immunity - A creature takes no damage of the specified type

FALL DAMAGE:
	A creature in an uncontrolled fall takes 1d6 of Physical damage for every 10ft they fall, upon impact.

SKILL CHECKS:
	Often, the GM will call for a Skill Check to determine how your character performs at a given task.
	These are typically resolved by rolling a d20, and adding your modifier for that skill.
	The total of this roll is compared to a "Difficulty Class".
	The Difficulty Class is a number determined by the GM that corresponds with the difficulty of the task your character is attempting.

ADVANTAGE & DISADVANTAGE:
	Sometimes, a particularly beneficial situation will grant advantage on a check or attack.
	To roll with advantage, roll two d20s and use the higher of the two when adding your modifier.
	Conversely, a negative effect or especially challenging situation will inflict disadvantage on a check.
	To roll with disadvantage, roll two d20s and use the lower of the two.

STACKING ADVANTAGE / DISADVANTAGE:
	If more than one beneficial circumstances converge to influence a check, you roll with double-advantage.
	For double-advantage, roll three d20s and take the highest.
	Conversely, with multiple problematic circumstances, you roll with double-disadvantage (roll three d20s and take the lowest).
	Advantage and disadvantage cancel each other out, one step at a time. 
	For example, if a situation that normally grants double-advantage occurs simultaneously with a situation that grants disadvantage, roll with normal advantage.

CRITICAL SUCCESSES AND FAILURES:
	Rolling a natural 20 on a d20 is a Critical Success: A Critical Success should produce an especially favorable result, whether narratively, mechanically, or both.
	Rolling a natural 1 on a d20 is a Critical Failure.: A Critical Failure should produce an especially unfavorable result, but does not automatically cause an extreme disaster.
	A Critical Success cannot make a truly impossible action possible.
	For details about how Critical Successes and Failures are applied in combat, see Chapter XX. 

DIFFICULTY CLASSES:
	This is a number set by the GM during a check, to determine how difficult it is for the check to succeed.
	The lower the number, the simpler the task is to succeed at. The higher the number, the more difficult.
	Example: DC 10 for climbing a rough wall, DC 25 for climbing a sheer, ice-covered cliff.
	DCs generally should not exceed 30 (however, the GM may choose to ignore this if appropriate).
	If a task is absolutely impossible by any means, no check should be called for at all.

SAVING THROWS:
	Sometimes, a check must be made to avoid a dangerous obstacle, a trap, or certain kinds of attacks.
	These are called Saving Throws, and are simply a d20 roll plus one of your character's Attributes (STR/DEX/CON/INT/WIS/CHA).
	Rolling a natural 20 on a Saving Throw means you avoid all damage and effects.
	Rolling a natural 1 on a Saving Throw is automatically a failure, even if an added modifier would normally make it a success.

CONTESTED CHECKS:
	When two characters directly oppose each other, both make an appropriate Check. The character with the higher result succeeds.
	If the results are tied, both characters must roll again and use the new roll.
	Advantage, Disadvantage, and other modifiers apply normally.
	Contested checks are one of the most common ways to resolve Wildcard actions (see Chapter XX).

INSPIRATION:
	If a player is especially clever, the GM may reward them with a Point of Inspiration.
	Examples of this type of cleverness include: skilled role-playing, ingenious solutions to puzzles of problems, exceptionally hilarious moments, or brilliant observations or deductions.
	Mark this Point of Inspiration on your character sheet.
	You can have up to 5 Points of Inspiration at any given time.
	Points of Inspiration can be spent to re-roll a failed Check or Attack Roll.

CONDITIONS:
	Conditions are temporary states that alter how a character can act, move, or resolve certain rolls. 
	A Condition remains in effect until its stated duration ends or its requirements for removal are met.
	By default, there are three Conditions: Exhausted, Prone, and Restrained. 

[CONDITION] EXHAUSTED:
	Exhaustion may be inflicted by prolonged physical exertion, sleep deprivation, extreme environmental conditions, or other circumstances that leave a character physically exhausted.
	An Exhausted character has Disadvantage on checks involving strenuous physical activity.
	A Long Rest removes the Exhausted condition.

[CONDITION] PRONE:
	If a combatant is on the ground, willingly or unwillingly, it has the "Prone" condition.
	A prone combatant can stand back up either by using an action, or by sacrificing half of their total number of squares of movement for that turn (rounded up).
	A prone character can crawl along the ground at half the rate of their normal Movement. 
	Melee attack rolls against a prone combatant may be granted Advantage.

[CONDITION] RESTRAINED:
	Certain moves, such as grappling an opponent, inflict the "Restrained" condition.
	A restrained creature’s speed becomes 0, and it can't benefit from any bonus to its speed.
	Attack rolls against the creature have advantage, and the creature’s attack rolls have disadvantage.
	The creature has disadvantage on Dexterity saving throws.

WEALTH:
	Your character's Wealth Level ranges from 1-10. (This takes the place of tracking individual currencies and coin counts.)
	The Game Master determines whether a purchase is reasonable for your Wealth Level.
	Unusually expensive purchases may reduce your Wealth Level.
	Wealth Level  1: Destitute. You struggle to afford basic necessities.
	Wealth Level  2: Poor. You can survive, but money is a constant concern.
	Wealth Level  3: Modest. You can afford ordinary necessities and minor comforts.
	Wealth Level  4: Comfortable. Routine expenses and common equipment are easily affordable.
	Wealth Level  5: Prosperous. You can afford quality goods, travel, and regular luxuries.
	Wealth Level  6: Wealthy. You have substantial resources, property, or income.
	Wealth Level  7: Very Wealthy. You can fund expeditions, employees, and major purchases.
	Wealth Level  8: Exceptionally Wealthy. You command resources comparable to nobles or major institutions.
	Wealth Level  9: Vast Fortune. You can finance armies, governments, or enormous projects.
	Wealth Level 10: Extreme Affluence. Only uniquely rare or world-changing purchases are beyond your reach.

EXPERIENCE:
	All player characters gain Levels at the same time.
	The GM awards a new Level when the group reaches an appropriate story milestone.
	For the mechanical effects and implications of gaining a Level, see Chapter XX.
