

WHAT IS A TTRPG?
	A tabletop roleplaying game is a shared storytelling game.
	Each player controls a character in an imagined world.
	One player takes the role of the Game Master.
	The rules and dice help determine what happens when the outcome is uncertain.

THE GAME MASTER
	The Game Master describes the world, its inhabitants, and the situations the characters encounter.
	The Game Master controls enemies, monsters, and other non-player characters.
	The Game Master decides when a dice roll is necessary.
	The Game Master interprets the rules and makes rulings when no exact rule applies.
	The Game Master is not the players' opponent. Their goal is to present interesting situations and help everyone create an enjoyable game.

THE PLAYERS
	Each player controls one main character.
	You describe what your character says, thinks, and attempts to do.
	You do not need to speak "in character" unless you want to.
	You are encouraged to ask questions about the situation before making decisions.
	Your character can attempt anything that makes sense, even if it is not explicitly on their character sheet or their cards.

THE BASIC FLOW OF PLAY
	1) The Game Master describes the situation.
	2) The players ask questions and describe what their characters do.
	3) The Game Master determines the results.
	4) Dice are rolled if the outcome is uncertain.
	5) The situation changes, and the process repeats.

THE FICTION COMES FIRST
	Always describe what your character is trying to accomplish. The rules are used to resolve that action.
	You do not necessarily need to name a specific Skill or game mechanic before describing what you do.
	The Game Master decides which rule or roll best fits the situation.

WHEN TO ROLL DICE
	Dice are rolled when an action could meaningfully succeed or fail.
	Trivially easy actions, or actions where the outcome is certain, usually do not require a roll.
	Impossible actions usually cannot be achieved by any roll at all. 
	The Game Master tells you what dice to roll and which modifier to add.

CHECKS
	A "Check" determines how well your character performs an uncertain task.
	Most checks are resolved with a standard procedure: roll a d20, add the relevant modifier, and compare the total to a Difficulty Class.
	The Difficulty Class is a number chosen by the DM that represents how challenging the task is to accomplish.
	If the total meets or exceeds the Difficulty Class, the Check succeeds.
	A higher result may produce a better outcome at the Game Master's discretion.
	Typically, rolling a 20 on the d20 (called a "Natural 20") means an exceptionally good result on your character's part (narratively, mechanically, or both). 
	Conversely, rolling a 1 on the d20 (a "Natural 1") may mean an exceptionally poor result, at the GM's discretion. 

ATTRIBUTES AND SKILLS
	Attributes describe your character's broad physical and mental capabilities.
	There are six attributes: Strength, Dexterity, Constitution, Intelligence, Wisdom, and Charisma.
	Skills describe their training in more specific areas.
	The Game Master decides whether an Attribute, Skill, or another modifier applies to a roll.
	Your character sheet lists the modifiers you add.

PLAYING YOUR CHARACTER
	Consider what your character wants and how they usually approach problems.
	You are not required to make the most efficient decision.
	Mistakes, risks, and unusual choices can make the game more interesting.
	Your character may disagree with other characters, but players should continue cooperating with each other.

WORKING AS A GROUP
	TTRPGs are cooperative games. Give other players room to speak and make decisions.
	Avoid deliberately disrupting the game or preventing others from participating.
	Share information your character would reasonably communicate.
	Help create opportunities for every character to contribute.

ASKING QUESTIONS
	You are always allowed to ask what your character can see, hear, know, or reasonably understand.
	You may ask the Game Master to clarify a rule or situation.
	If you are unsure what to do, ask the group for suggestions.
	Calling a Timeout pauses play so the group can resolve confusion or discomfort.

FAILURE
	Failure does not mean you played incorrectly.
	A failed roll may introduce danger, complications, lost time, or difficult choices.
	Failure should usually move the situation forward rather than stop the game entirely.
	Characters are expected to face setbacks.

COMBAT
	Combat begins when the Game Master determines that turn-by-turn action is necessary.
	Combat uses a grid, Initiative, Actions, Ability cards, and other rules described later in this book.
	On your turn, decide what your character is trying to accomplish and use your Actions accordingly.
	You may ask other players for help understanding your options.

YOU DO NOT NEED TO KNOW EVERYTHING
	You are not expected to memorize the entire rulebook.
	Learn the basic rules that apply to your character and refer to the book when necessary.
	More experienced players may help explain rules, but the Game Master makes the final ruling.
	The best way to learn OSK is by playing.
